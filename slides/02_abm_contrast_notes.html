Speaker Notes — Block 2: Agents, Networks, and the Geometry of Interaction

Agent-Based Modeling for Statisticians — JSM 2026

John S. Schuler

Slide 1 — Where We Left Off

Reconnecting After the Break

This slide orients returning audience members. The key move is to remind them that what follows is not a different subject — it is the same question asked with a different instrument. The mean-field assumption was identified precisely in Block 1; Block 2 relaxes it and shows what becomes visible.

Slide 2 — Roadmap

Block 2: Agents, Networks, and the Geometry of Interaction

Set the tone immediately: this is not an argument that ODE models are wrong. It is a demonstration that they are a special case of a richer language. The statistician analogy that will carry the session is regularization — the ODE is the ABM with a very strong prior. Do not state this yet. Let it arrive after the reduction result is shown.

Slide 3

What Is an Agent?

The neighborhood concept is the load-bearing idea. Everything else follows from it. In the ODE, the neighborhood is implicit and uniform — the mass-action term embeds it without naming it. In the ABM, it is explicit and potentially heterogeneous. This makes it a first-class object: something you can vary, model, and test hypotheses about. Statisticians will recognize the analogy to specifying a covariance structure — the ODE is like assuming independence, the ABM lets you specify the structure.

Slide 4

ABM Lotka-Volterra: The Rules

The rules are deliberately verbal before they are computational. This is intentional — the verbal statement is the model; the code is just the execution. A model that can only be described in code is a model that cannot be criticized. These three rules map directly to the three terms in the Lotka-Volterra ODEs: alpha×N, beta×N×P, gamma×P. The correspondence will be made visible in the reduction result.

Slide 5

ABM Lotka-Volterra: Core Code

The code structure mirrors the verbal rules exactly. rbinom calls make the stochasticity explicit. Rule 2 is the mean-field assumption in code: each predator independently samples every prey individual with probability p_kill. The expected number of kills is p_kill × N × P = β N P — the functional form βNP written procedurally. p_kill maps directly to β; p_kill × p_convert maps to δ.

Slide 6 — Run loop slide

ABM Lotka-Volterra: Run Function

The early termination condition — if (nrow(...) == 0) break — is doing something the ODE cannot: it is detecting extinction. The ODE trajectory would continue through zero and into negative population sizes without complaint. The ABM respects the absorbing boundary.

Slide 7

The ODE Is Not a Different Model

State this before showing the reduction plot, not after. If you show the convergence first and then explain it, it looks like post-hoc justification. If you explain it first, the plot arrives as confirmation of a prediction. This is the rhetorical structure of the reduction result: the ABM is the more general model, and the ODE falls out of it as a theorem. Not as an approximation — as a theorem.

Slide 8

The Reduction Result

The solid line is the ODE. The translucent traces are ten ABM runs with different random seeds. At this population size, the ABM orbits near the ODE solution — but notice the variance. Some runs track closely; a few diverge. The divergence will become the subject of the next slide. The key message: convergence is real, but it requires large N and random mixing. Both conditions are assumptions of the ODE.

Slide 9

The ODE Cannot See This

Some of the translucent lines hit zero and stay there — extinction. The ODE line continues cycling, because a differential equation defined at the origin has no mechanism for absorbing state dynamics. The ODE is not wrong about the populations it was designed to describe — large, well-mixed, continuous. It is incapable of expressing a phenomenon that requires discreteness and small-number stochasticity. The ODE cannot say “the population went extinct.” The phrase is grammatically forbidden in its language.

Slide 10

ABM SIR: Random Mixing

The infection mechanism implements frequency-dependent transmission: each susceptible is independently exposed with probability beta×I/N, so expected new infections = S × beta × I / N — the discrete-time counterpart of the ODE’s beta×S×I/N term. This is the mean-field assumption in agent code: every susceptible is equally reachable by every infectious agent. Recovery is a Bernoulli trial with probability gamma, giving geometrically distributed infectious periods — the discrete analog of the ODE’s exponential recovery.

Slide 11

ABM SIR: Convergence to ODE

The convergence is clear at N = 500. The ABM runs cluster tightly around the ODE solution — peak timing, peak height, and tail behavior all align. The residual variance is demographic stochasticity: the irreducible randomness of a finite population. As N grows, this variance shrinks and the ABM approaches the ODE exactly. This is the reduction result for SIR.

Slide 12

Now Give Them an Address

The transition here is from the ODE’s abstraction (the fully mixed population) to a structure that looks more like how transmission actually works. The key point is not that contact networks are “more realistic” — it is that they introduce a new structural object, the network topology, which is not representable in the ODE’s language. Network topology is a first-class variable in this language. You can ask questions about it. You can vary it and observe the consequences.

Slide 13

Two Networks, Same Mean Degree

Mean degree is fixed to isolate the effect of topology on epidemic dynamics. Everything else — transmission rate, recovery rate, initial infected fraction — will be identical. If the ODE were the correct model, we would see identical epidemic curves. What we will see is something quite different.

Slide 14

The Networks: Erdős-Rényi vs. Barabási-Albert

The visual difference is immediate and striking. In the Erdős-Rényi graph, nodes are roughly uniform in size — similar degrees throughout. In the Barabási-Albert graph, a few large nodes (hubs) dominate. These hubs are connected to many other nodes. In an epidemic, they are both highly likely to be infected early (many contacts) and highly efficient at spreading infection further. The ODE sees only mean degree. It is blind to the variance.

Slide 15

Erdős-Rényi vs. Barabási-Albert: Degree Distribution

The policy implication of the Barabási-Albert topology is significant: targeted vaccination of hubs is dramatically more efficient than random vaccination, because hubs carry a disproportionate share of the transmission. The ODE cannot represent this strategy. The network SIR can. This is not a matter of fit — it is a matter of the question being askable.

Slide 16

Network SIR: Edge-Traversal Infection

The critical structural change from the random-mixing ABM: infection now traverses edges only. adj[[i]] gives the neighbors of node i; susceptibles among them are the only candidates for transmission. The ODE’s βSI term is replaced by edge-by-edge Bernoulli trials. The contact structure is now load-bearing, not abstracted away.

Slide 17

Same \(\mathcal{R}_0\). Different World.

Both networks have the same mean degree and the same transmission parameters. The ODE predicts one epidemic curve. These networks produce qualitatively different dynamics: the Barabási-Albert epidemic peaks earlier, shows more variance across runs (because hub-seeding is a random event), and can sustain longer tails through hub persistence. The ODE’s answer is neither of these — it is the mean-field average, which corresponds to neither topology. The question “what does contact network topology do to epidemic dynamics?” cannot be asked in the ODE’s language. The answer it would give is not wrong. The question is simply exterior to its expressive range.

Slide 18

The Reduction Principle

This is the central theoretical claim of the session. State it with confidence. The Bayesian framing is available here if the audience wants it: the ODE is the posterior mode of the ABM under a Dirac prior on the interaction structure. The ABM relaxes that prior. The phenomena visible only in the ABM are those that have probability zero under the ODE’s prior — not unlikely, but grammatically excluded. Regime change, extinction, network effects, spatial heterogeneity: all exterior to the language.

Slide 19

Nominal Parameters vs. Effective Dimensions

This is the answer to the most common technical objection to ABMs — placed here, after the full demonstration, so it answers a question the audience has already formed rather than anticipating one they haven’t. The Bayesian framing: maximal regularization corresponds to a prior concentrated entirely on the mean-field interaction structure — one that assigns essentially all mass to random mixing. That is not parameter parsimony; it is a structural restriction that has been silently imposed.

Slide 20

Spatial Wolf-Sheep Predation (Extension Module)

The image is the argument. The ODE with these parameters produces neutral cycles indefinitely. The ABM — same parameters, local interaction — produces boom-and-bust dynamics that terminate in extinction. The difference is not a quantitative deviation but a qualitative one: the absorbing state at zero is grammatically excluded from the ODE’s language. Once populations hit zero in the ABM, there is no recovery. The ODE has no word for this.

Slide 21 — Spatial code slide

Wolf-Sheep: Code Overview

The key structural change from the random-mixing ABM is one line: wolves eat sheep-here — sheep on the same cell — not a random draw from the full population. That single restriction replaces the mean-field assumption with a local one. The rest of the model is standard energy bookkeeping. Show this code briefly, then switch to the pre-rendered image.

Slide 22 — Animation slide

[Spatial] Wolf-Sheep Predation

Six snapshots from a single run. The ODE with equivalent parameters cycles forever. This ABM — same birth rates, same predation rates, local interaction only — crashes to zero at t = 146. Extinction is an absorbing state. The ODE cannot represent it because its language has no symbol for zero population; the differential equation simply continues through zero and into the negative. The absorbing boundary is exterior to the language. This is what “inexpressible” means.

Slide 23 — Time series slide

[Spatial] Wolf-Sheep — Population Over Time

The same run, now shown as a time series. Sheep (blue) multiply unchecked at first; wolves (red) follow them up with a lag. At around t = 110 the wolves overshoot — 544 wolves, only 195 sheep remaining. The prey base collapses, and the wolves, now starving on an empty grid, die off by t = 146. The ODE with these parameters cycles forever. The ABM does not. The difference is the absorbing boundary at zero — a state the ODE cannot represent.

Slide 24

What If Sheep Need to Eat?

The grass extension is not about realism for its own sake. It is about identifying which assumptions are load-bearing. The no-grass model assumes the prey resource is inexhaustible — exactly the assumption embedded in the ODE’s linear prey growth term. Adding grass makes that assumption explicit and relaxes it.

Slide 25 — Parallel runs slide

Cycling Is Sparse Without Grass

50 independent runs of each model variant, 300 ticks. Without grass (left), 47 of 50 runs end in wolf overshoot followed by sheep extinction — the boom-bust dynamic terminates. The ODE’s cyclic solution is the rare outcome, not the typical one. Adding a third trophic level — grass that regenerates at a fixed rate — converts the picture: 49 of 50 grass runs sustain both species to t = 300. The grass acts as a resource buffer: when sheep are scarce, grass accumulates; recovered grass supports sheep recovery; sheep recovery supports wolf recovery. The ODE cycles forever by assumption. The no-grass ABM shows that assumption is doing real work. It is not a neutral modeling choice — it is the condition under which cycling is actually possible.

Slide 26

Block 2: Summary

The summary should feel like a crystallization, not a list. The key points to land: (1) the competition between ODE and ABM is false — one contains the other; (2) the expressive boundary is the operative concept; (3) the statistician’s instinct toward model parsimony is correct, but parsimony should be measured in effective dimensions, not parameter counts; (4) the phenomena visible only in the ABM are exactly those that require the features the ODE’s language excludes.

Slide 27 — Transition to Block 3

Toward Axtell

The transition to Block 3 should feel like continuity, not a change of subject. The same conceptual move — identifying phenomena exterior to a language — now applies to a different domain: the distribution of firm sizes. The audience has seen the argument work twice (extinction, network topology). They are prepared to accept it a third time. The empirical stability of the power law is what makes the Axtell example devastating: this is not a modeling artifact. It is a regularity in the world, and the standard languages cannot express it.
